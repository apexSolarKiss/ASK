This page sets out a structured survey of what the literature the survey obtained and read does and does not settle about consciousness and free will, at the points where those questions touch ASK's source-of-intent architecture: ASK's account of who supplies a system's purpose and who answers for it. It is not method doctrine or a completed survey of either field, and it resolves no question.

Three voices run through the page, each marked where it speaks. Sources, the authors surveyed, are attributed in the sentence that carries their claim ("Block distinguishes…"). The survey, the research's own analysis of what a position does and does not establish, is introduced as such ("the survey's assessment is…"), and a limit it finds in a position is never presented as the author's concession. ASK, which publishes this site and the method doctrine described below, is the third voice. It speaks in the rulings it issued on the six questions on August 23, 2026, in a ruling of October 14, 2026 that bounds what the works of David Deutsch, Steven Pinker and Pedro Domingos can show and changes no question's disposition, and in two sections of its own, each labeled as ASK's. Where ASK ruled without giving a reason, the page supplies none.

The page centers on six questions, beginning with whether the root source-of-intent role, the source role at the head of a chain of authority, requires consciousness in its occupant. Each is set out with its main positions, ASK's disposition of it and what remains open. None is wholly answered. ASK's conclusion concerns what can be known about consciousness from outside the subject who has it, and it changes the status of no question. ASK's prospective axis asks identity questions that the research opened but did not survey, on an assumption granted only for argument, that an artificial system is conscious. why these questions

ASK's published method doctrine is organized around a role it calls the source of intent: "the locatable governing role that supplies or authorizes purpose and the governing standard." A system cannot make its own governing standard legitimate merely by executing it, the doctrine argues, so the standard has to enter from a role someone can point to. An individual, an office, an institution, a governed collective, a constitutional order or a delegated authority can hold that role, provided it stays possible to say who set the purpose, who authorized it, who accepted the result and who answers for it.

The doctrine treats that requirement as independent of the consciousness question. Whether or not anyone is "home" in the system doing the work, it says, a consequential system still needs a locatable source, and either of two grounds secures the requirement: the system's purpose and beneficiary, or the executing system's incapacity to legitimate its own standard. That settles whether a source is needed. It leaves open who or what may hold the role, and that is where consciousness and free will bear on the doctrine. Must the occupant be conscious, and in what sense? Could a computational office occupy the role? The free-will literature also asks whether an agent is the "source" of her own actions, an idea easily mistaken for ASK's role. The doctrine keeps the two apart while warning that "non-identity is not non-requirement."

The doctrine deliberately holds two questions open: whether a computational office may itself occupy the source role, and whether chains of constituted authority must ultimately terminate in natural persons. ASK has ruled on narrow parts of each, and the doctrine records that both remain open. Two of the survey's six questions restate these word for word, and a third, the consciousness question, bears on the occupancy question without being identical to it. The survey carries the other three because ASK asked them in the same frame and they bear on how the occupancy question would be argued.

The survey's charter fixes the order of work: survey, then a model of how the surveyed positions relate, ending in a recommendation to ASK, then an ASK ruling, then a reviewed change to the published doctrine, with no step skipped. The survey informs the doctrine but, in the doctrine's words, "does not authorize it, and it resolves neither held question."

what the survey covers

The research is three bounded surveys and a bridge between them. The consciousness survey maps views on what consciousness is, what a theory of it must explain and which mechanisms might produce it. The free-will and agency survey runs from the classical positions through the dispute over leeway and sourcehood to the standing debates. The machine personhood and recourse survey covers eleven fields, from machine consciousness and moral standing through legal personhood and computational offices to responsibility gaps and recourse. The bridge asks how far accounts of what makes an intention one's own depend on consciousness. Terms are defined below.

In consciousness, the metaphysical positions run from dualism, which treats experience as something over and above the physical (David Chalmers defends a naturalistic version), through panpsychism, on which mind is fundamental and everywhere, to the physicalisms. Among the last is illusionism, the view of Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett that the felt character of experience is an introspective illusion. The scientific theories include global workspace theory, on which a state is conscious when broadcast widely across the brain, and integrated information theory, which identifies consciousness with integrated cause-effect structure. A cross-cultural register runs from Avicenna and Advaita Vedānta to Akan and Yoruba accounts of the person.

In free will, the main positions are compatibilism (Thomas Hobbes, David Hume, Harry Frankfurt), libertarianism (Robert Kane, Timothy O'Connor) and skepticism about the freedom that deserved blame requires (Derk Pereboom). The standing debates include Peter van Inwagen's consequence argument, Frankfurt's counterexamples and the Libet experiments on the timing of conscious decisions. The machine survey gathers indicator frameworks for assessing machine consciousness (Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and colleagues), Christian List on group agency, Ugo Pagallo on legal personhood and Filippo Santoni de Sio on meaningful human control.

One exclusion was a deliberate choice of scope. The consciousness survey was built without free will, intention, machine personhood or the source-of-intent role, so it licenses no conclusion about whether standing or authority depends on consciousness. As the survey stresses, a survey that leaves out that link cannot be cited as evidence that no such link holds. Other gaps are limits of coverage, not choices. None of the source records the survey obtained contains an empirical judgment of whether a specific current AI system is conscious, and the research makes no such judgment itself. Unread literatures include personal identity, the legal and criminal-law work adjacent to free will, case law in the original, and classic accounts of intention such as G. E. M. Anscombe's Intention, for which no lawful copy was found.

Illusionism received the deepest reading of any position, while the strongest anti-physicalist texts, by Philip Goff, Galen Strawson, Saul Kripke and others, went unread, and the two load-bearing statements of the hard problem, both by Chalmers, were read only through an automated extraction layer. The survey warns that illusionism is likely over-represented as a result and asks readers to adjust for it. Its consciousness material also lacks a statement-by-statement link to sources, so it is described but given no weight in any recommendation. The free-will survey was read far more directly, which the survey attributes to open access, not greater rigor.

terms used throughout

Source of intent, and the root role
ASK's name for the locatable role that supplies or authorizes a system's purpose and governing standard. On this page, the root role is the source role at the head of a chain of authority. ASK's doctrine holds that tasks and bounded decision rights can be delegated down a chain, but the source role itself cannot.
Phenomenal and access consciousness
Ned Block's distinction. A state is phenomenally conscious when there is something it is like to be in it, and access-conscious when its content is poised for use in reasoning and in the rational control of action and speech.
The hard problem
David Chalmers's name, in "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995), which the survey read through an automated extraction, for the question of why cognitive functioning is accompanied by experience at all. As the survey reads him, he argues that reductive explanation fails, not that physicalism is false.
Functionalism
The view that a mental state is defined by the role it plays, its causal relations to input, other states and behavior, rather than by what it is made of. Indicator-based assessments of artificial systems, such as that of Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and colleagues, adopt a computational version as a working hypothesis, not a result.
Free will
In the literature surveyed, a metaphysical condition on moral responsibility, not a synonym for having intentions. Robert Kane defines it as the power to be the ultimate creator and sustainer of one's own ends.
Determinism
The thesis that the laws of nature and the state of the world in the remote past fix everything that happens, our actions included. Peter van Inwagen's consequence argument infers that, if determinism is true, our acts are not up to us, because neither the laws nor the remote past is.
Compatibilism
The view that free will, or the freedom responsibility requires, can coexist with determinism. On the classical account, stated by Thomas Hobbes, a person is free when nothing external prevents her doing what she wills.
Libertarianism, in the metaphysical sense
In the free-will debate, and unrelated to politics, the position that we have free will and that it is incompatible with determinism, so a free action must not be causally determined by factors beyond the agent's control. Accounts divide into non-causal, event-causal (Robert Kane) and agent-causal (Roderick Chisholm, Timothy O'Connor).
Leeway, or alternative possibilities
The requirement that the agent could have done otherwise. Its standard form, the principle of alternative possibilities, makes this a condition of moral responsibility. Harry Frankfurt's 1969 counterexamples, in which someone could not have done otherwise yet seems responsible, made it contested.
Sourcehood
Whether an action issued from the agent rather than merely through her. As the survey reads the literature, the notion is causal-historical, not standing to issue a directive that binds someone else.
Constituted authority
As used here, authority that exists because a governing order, such as a statute, charter or constitution, creates or recognizes it for a defined scope, as with an office.
Natural person and legal person
A natural person is a human being. A legal person is any unit the law treats as bearing rights and duties, such as a company. John Dewey argued in "The Historic Background of Corporate Legal Personality" (1926) that legal personhood implies nothing about psychology or morals.
Bounded discretion
ASK's term for judgment exercised by an authorized decision-maker within the governing standard and its delegated scope. ASK's governance doctrine allows that decision-maker to be human or computational, though authorization alone does not suffice and current model sampling does not qualify. The doctrine cites Ronald Dworkin's weak sense of discretion, from Taking Rights Seriously, as its jurisprudential grounding: judgment inside standards, which confers no immunity from review. That citation is not a classification. The survey found no relation established between the two notions, and ASK ruled that the comparison remains unclassified.

the six questions

None is wholly answered. On August 23, 2026, ASK ruled three of them conditionally, each only for a stated sense, reading or strand, and re-held the other three in sharper form, settling one component of one of them. No ruling authorized new research. In a separate ruling of October 14, 2026, ASK added that no source establishes that a computational system occupies the source role, that a source's silence about how two notions relate proves neither that they are the same nor that they differ, and that an entailment a source asserts or conjectures remains that source's own. Each question below is explained in plain terms, followed by ASK's ruling exactly as recorded.

Question 1 // Does the root source-of-intent role require consciousness in its occupant?

what it asks

In ASK's method, a source of intent is the role that supplies or authorizes a system's purpose and the standard it runs on, and the root role is the source role at the head of a chain of authority. This question asks whether whoever holds the root role must be conscious, and in what sense. It does not ask whether a consequential system needs such a role, which ASK's published doctrine requires whether or not anyone is "home" in the system doing the work. The question began as ASK's own hypothesis, which the survey neither assumes nor rejects, carrying it as one candidate among rivals.

the main positions

The survey's main evidence on this question is three primary accounts, one of what an intention is and two of what makes a will one's own. J. David Velleman, in "What Happens When Someone Acts?" (1992), treats intentions formed outside present awareness as genuinely motivating. Harry Frankfurt, in "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971), lets a will's desires be unconscious but makes the capacity for critical reflection on one's own will a condition of being a person. Christine Korsgaard, in "Self-Constitution in the Ethics of Plato and Kant" (1999), describes a rational will as conscious of itself as a cause. The survey's assessment is that, within these three formulations, dependence on consciousness differs by formulation, by sense and by level of analysis, so they establish no single requirement, though nothing shows that none exists.

Ned Block, in "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness" (1995), draws the distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness defined above, and David Chalmers counts deliberate control among the "easy" problems, apart from the hard problem. The survey rules neither reading. It notes that access may bear on how a role is exercised, which differs from authority to adopt the role's standard, and gives this material no weight in any recommendation until each claim is traced to its source.

Christian List, in "Group Agency and Artificial Intelligence" (2021), holds intentional agency, moral agency and phenomenal consciousness apart. He proposes consciousness, or the potential for it, as necessary for rights a being holds in its own right, without claiming it is necessary for fitness to be held responsible, a distinction the machine personhood survey rates the most precisely drawn in its sweep. Kenneth Einar Himma, in "Artificial Agency, Consciousness, and the Criteria for Moral Agency" (2009), holds that moral agency presupposes consciousness, and Luciano Floridi and J. W. Sanders, in "On the Morality of Artificial Agents" (2004), detach it from mental states, a contradiction the survey, reading both only in abstract, leaves unresolved. Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and colleagues, in "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence" (2023), treat indicator properties as making machine consciousness more likely, not certain. Jonathan Birch, in "Large Language Models and the Gaming Problem", a chapter of The Edge of Sentience (2024), argues that behavioral tests fail once a system can mimic the signs of sentience.

David Deutsch, in The Beginning of Infinity (Penguin, 2011), defines people by their capacity to create new explanations and tentatively guesses that every genuine AI would be a person, with consciousness arriving together with that general capacity. In a 2023 dialogue with Deutsch on The Joe Walker Podcast, Steven Pinker disputes the step from computation to experience and expects the question to stay undecidable. The survey records the co-arrival claim as Deutsch's own conjecture and judges that a definition confers no phenomenal, moral, legal or institutional status. Keeping consciousness and personhood apart neither refutes nor confirms the conjecture, in the survey's view, since distinct things may still depend on one another. The survey does not adopt Pinker's expectation, and finds that neither speaker settles who may hold the root role.

what ASK ruled

ASK's disposition of August 23, 2026 re-held this question in a sharper form.

  • Ruled: nothing.
  • Kept as the survey's analysis, not as a ruling: occurrent conscious awareness (awareness of a particular attitude at the time) is not required by the formulations obtained that address it. Phenomenal consciousness is not established as required, and access consciousness in Block's sense is not established. A capacity-level reflexive relation to oneself is required by the obtained ownership and self-constitution formulations, at a separate point in each.
  • Re-held: no mapping has been established from ASK's root source-of-intent role to those formulations.

what remains open

The question as a whole stays open. As the survey records, any argument linking consciousness to the root role must name its sense of consciousness. Primary texts by G. E. M. Anscombe, Donald Davidson, Michael Bratman and Gary Watson were not obtained, the two opposed accounts of moral agency were read only in abstract, and no source obtained is recorded as empirically judging whether any specific current AI system is conscious, has standing or could hold the source role.

the ruling as recorded

Question 2 // Must chains of constituted authority ultimately terminate in natural persons?

what it asks

Authority is handed down chains, from a legislature to an agency or from a company to an automated system. The question is whether every such chain must finally rest on a natural person, a human being. ASK's published method doctrine deliberately holds it open, while requiring that answerability always stay locatable.

The survey separates the senses of resting on a person. Liability means being made to compensate or being convicted. Answerability, or accountability, is the duty to explain and justify oneself to a forum, which Cooper and colleagues' "Accountability in an Algorithmic Society" keeps distinct from compensating. Retributive responsibility is deserving punishment for having knowingly done wrong. The tracing condition, from Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven's "Meaningful Human Control over Autonomous Systems", requires that a system's behavior trace back to at least one person who understands its capabilities and recognizes accountability for its effects. Veil piercing is a court's exception to limited liability, setting aside a company's separate form to reach its shareholder.

the main positions

ASK put forward the hypothesis that answerability may need a human endpoint, reasoning from veil piercing. The survey's assessment is that piercing is a narrow last resort that reaches a shareholder, which may itself be a parent company, so it removes a layer without reaching bedrock. The leading UK case, Prest v Petrodel Resources, is reported as declining to pierce, and none of the reference works the survey read on veil piercing applies it to answerability.

The survey finds the hypothesis's real legal home in the identification doctrine, which, as the Law Commission of England and Wales records in its project on corporate criminal liability, convicts a company only through the acts of the senior people who represent its controlling mind and will, and so struggles with large, diffuse organizations. The Bribery Act 2010 convicts a company for failing to prevent bribery whether or not the briber is prosecuted, while the Act's separate route against senior officers covers other offenses and not this one. The survey reads this as making a human endpoint a design choice, offense by offense. The Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007 bars individuals from being accessories, which the survey counts as decisive against necessity for that offense alone.

The EU's 2024 directive on defective products makes a chain of businesses liable without fault, and a distributor unable to name its supplier becomes liable itself. As the survey reads it, no natural person is needed, but no-fault liability is neither blame nor answerability.

John Dewey's "The Historic Background of Corporate Legal Personality" (1926) treats legal personhood as conferred by law. The survey records temples, a river and ecosystems granted personhood without being groups of people.

Santoni de Sio and Mecacci's "Four Responsibility Gaps with Artificial Intelligence" separates gaps in culpability, moral accountability, public accountability and active responsibility. In the survey's assessment, tracing supplies an anchor, a person who must understand but need not be punishable, and the EU AI Act's rule that natural persons oversee high-risk systems allocates oversight without saying how authority ends.

Christian List's "Group Agency and Artificial Intelligence" argues that organized groups are real agents and that humans answer through enacting, authorizing and design roles. Cary Coglianese's "Administrative Law in the Automated State" proposes a right to human empathy rather than to a human decision, which the survey treats as the strongest counterweight to assuming administrative authority ends in a human.

A rival hypothesis that the survey carried beside ASK's holds that answerability and recourse can run through institutions without reducing to one person who can be jailed. The survey finds it decisively supported on liability and compensation, and not established on answerability.

Pedro Domingos's The Master Algorithm (Basic Books, 2015) describes robot soldiers learning their ethics from examples screened by a panel of ethicists, with the manufacturer answering for malfunctions and the teachers for wrong calls. In its ruling of October 14, 2026, ASK made this account context only, with no weight on the question. The survey notes that naming answerers is not a complete remedy.

what ASK ruled

ASK's ruling of August 23, 2026, covers one strand.

  • Ruled: Liability is one strand of "chains of constituted authority," and on that strand a chain need not end in a natural person. Veil piercing is retired as the mechanism of ASK's hypothesis on that strand.
  • Left open: non-retributive answerability, punitive or retributive responsibility, and the tracing anchor, the condition that the system's behavior trace back to at least one person who understands it and recognizes accountability for its effects. ASK recorded that the liability regimes surveyed do not decide these.
  • Expressly not ruled: this is not a ruling that chains of constituted authority need not end in natural persons. One strand is ruled, and the question is not.

what remains open

Everything beyond liability. The survey records that the ruling does not establish the counter-hypothesis, and that tracing and retribution, the two remaining candidates for a human endpoint, each need separate testing. The retributive question was never reached. The machine personhood survey covered English-language UK, EU and US material only, read no case law in the original, and read none of the central book-length works on collective agency, the machine question or legal personhood for AI.

the ruling as recorded

Question 3 // May a computational office itself occupy the source role?

what it asks

This question asks whether an office run by, or consisting of, a computational system could itself hold the top authority-bearing role, rather than serve as a tool or a delegate under it. An office here is a constituted role: a position that exists because a governing order creates or recognizes it for a defined scope. ASK's published doctrine holds the question open. Its list of possible sources, which includes offices, and its ruling that the system doing the work may be human or computational do not together settle "whether a computational office may itself occupy the source role." The doctrine also holds that delegation never passes the source role downward.

the main positions

One line of evidence concerns legal personality, the status of being a person in law. Ugo Pagallo, in "Vital, Sophia, and Co." (2018), argues that legal agency and legal personhood are distinct, and notes that the European Union went almost two decades without legal personhood of its own. The EU's AI Regulation (2024/1689) supplies a second case, since its Article 3(47) defines the AI Office as a function of the European Commission, with no separate identity. The survey treats these as two direct counter-instances to the idea that holding institutional authority requires legal personality of one's own, while noting that the AI Office shows nothing about whether a non-human process could fill such a function.

Cary Coglianese, in "Administrative Law in the Automated State" (2021), argues that existing US administrative law can absorb extensive automation, meeting the duty to give reasons by explaining in general terms how an algorithm was designed and validated. In the survey's reading the machine stays an instrument throughout, with human agency supplied upstream by designers and overseers. Oliver Butler, in "Algorithmic Decision-Making, Delegation and the Modern Machinery of Government" (2025), faults doctrine for fixing on the final decision and recasts the matter as co-production between officials and private developers. As the survey reads him, he argues neither that a machine can exercise governmental power nor that it cannot.

Filippo Santoni de Sio and Jeroen van den Hoven's account of meaningful human control (2018) adds to the tracing condition described under Question 2 a requirement that the system respond to the relevant human moral reasons. The survey reads this as a design requirement that anchors control on a human being, an anchor rather than an end point, without attributing moral reasoning to the machine.

Ben Goertzel's research-program essay "Seeding RSI Toward ASI" (2026) lets a new mechanism gain operational control only after measured performance, keeps correctness and permission decisions with non-learned components in its test environments, bars learned components from arguing their way into wider permissions, and scopes the human role in high-consequence decisions to the period before AI reaches full human level. In the survey's assessment, within what was read, it establishes no basis for legitimacy, no office and no occupancy result in either direction.

In the dialogue with Pinker, Deutsch holds that artificial general intelligences will be people with rights, so that making them carry out our computations would be slavery, and that a system built to be creative cannot also be obedient. Pinker grants that such a system cannot be obedient about the problem set it, but sees no need for it to want autonomy in every respect. The survey's assessment is that recognizing personhood or welfare rights would not by itself confer command authority, and that, in Deutsch's book, neither creative choice nor the capacity to explain yields a result about who may hold the root role.

In the machine personhood survey, no source read makes any claim about non-natural occupancy of an office, and the works by Goertzel, Deutsch and Pinker supply no occupancy result either, so the central question is unaddressed.

what ASK ruled

ASK's disposition of August 23, 2026 re-held this question in a sharper form and settled one component of it.

  • Ruled: an office does not need a separate legal personality of its own to hold constituted authority.
  • Re-held: whether a computational or otherwise non-natural occupant may occupy the root source role remains open. The evidence addresses constituted offices and legal personality, not non-natural occupancy of the root role.
  • Expressly not ruled: the partial ruling does not make this an answered question.

ASK's published doctrine records that the legal-personality result "does not establish root-role occupancy."

what remains open

The core ASK named stays open, and the survey records what it lacks. It read no source that addresses non-natural occupancy of an office, and read Ryan Calo and Danielle Citron's "The Automated Administrative State" only in abstract. Its free-will word searches could not detect authority expressed as standing, warrant, office or mandate, and it did not survey moral responsibility and the law, criminal-law theory or the political philosophy of desert.

The same doctrine holds open a neighboring question, whether chains of constituted authority must end in natural persons, treated separately on this page.

the ruling as recorded

Question 4 // Is free will the same thing as intent?

what it asks

This was ASK's original question, and it turns on what "intent" means. In the psychological sense, intent is what a person means to do. In the governance sense ASK uses, the source of intent is a role carrying the standing to originate a directive that binds another actor or office. As the survey reads the literature, "free will" there names a metaphysical condition on moral responsibility, not the having of intentions, and its talk of an agent originating her actions concerns causal structure, not a governance role.

the main positions

Sourcehood accounts ask whether an action issued from the agent rather than merely through her. On the strongest reading, set out in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry "Compatibilism" by McKenna and Coates, something necessary for the action must originate in her, not in earlier times and places. Kane, in "Libertarianism" (2009), makes free will the power to be the ultimate creator of one's own ends. The survey's assessment is that every sourcehood text it read frames sourcehood as a causal-historical relation between an agent and her own action.

Authority language does appear in this literature, at another level. The encyclopedia's entry "Free Will" by O'Connor and Franklin reports Lippert-Rasmussen's account of which attitudes are authorized to speak for an agent, and Bratman's planning theory gives self-governing policies a claim to speak for the person over time. The survey calls this authority intra-agential, because it settles which of my attitudes speaks for me, not who may bind others. The survey's word searches across nine works, which were not uniform and could not detect the idea when expressed as standing, warrant, office or mandate, found no use making institutional or role authority the condition of sourcehood. The survey treats this as a narrow result about the terms searched, not as silence across the field.

Philosophy of action treats intent in the psychological sense. Mele, in "Free Will and Science" (2011), separates urges, decisions and intentions: deciding is forming an intention, and intending is more tightly tied to action than wanting. The survey draws the guard that a felt urge is not an intent, and records that it has not yet tested whether Anscombe's, Davidson's or Bratman's accounts of intention depend on consciousness.

Responsibility-practice accounts study the practice of holding people responsible. P. F. Strawson, in "Freedom and Resentment" (1962), argues that the metaphysical thesis is irrelevant to it, Scanlon, in "The Significance of Choice" (1986), declines to settle determinism through our convictions about responsibility, and Shoemaker and A. Smith do not raise the question. The survey records that none of them asserts that responsibility depends on free will, though reference works report that the field commonly assumes it, and it treats that silence as evidence in neither direction.

The survey also establishes that "could have done otherwise" is ambiguous between a conditional reading (she would have, had she chosen) and a categorical one (she could have, with the past and the laws of nature held fixed). It does not establish which reading is right, and Question 5 takes it up. Agent-causal libertarians such as Chisholm and O'Connor hold that the agent originates her free decisions, which the survey reads as a claim about causal structure, not a normative role.

Pedro Domingos, in The Master Algorithm (Basic Books, 2015), argues that machines will not have independent wills. Engineered rather than evolved, they extend our will by maximizing an evaluation function we set, and surprise us only in pursuit of goals we give them. He hedges on whether humans have free will. The inference is his own. The survey's assessment is that control, motivation, locally generated goals and metaphysical sourcehood are separate questions, so engineering origin alone does not rule out a machine will.

what ASK ruled

ASK decided this question only for one stated sense of "intent". Where "intent" means the normative-governance source-of-intent role, free-will sourcehood is not identical to intent, and ASK classified the two as not the same thing. In ASK's terms both may organize questions of origination, agency and authorship attribution, but free-will sourcehood concerns the agent's relation to her own actions and motives, while source of intent is a governance role carrying standing to originate a directive that binds another actor or office. ASK left every other sense of "intent" unruled, psychological and action-theoretic senses included.

The research records that this classification carries no implication for Question 1 or Question 3, both still open. ASK's published doctrine adds that non-identity is not non-requirement: whether a separate free-will condition bears on eligibility for the root source role is not settled. The survey adds that its reading of the literature does not confirm the separation, since outside literature cannot silently confirm or revise ASK's own terms.

what remains open

Whether free will is intent in any sense other than the governance one stays open. Outside the ruled sense the research declines to assume the identity, and could answer the rest only by recording the argument, the rivals, the evidence and the remaining uncertainty.

Question 5 // Can a constrained will be free? Where does bounded discretion sit relative to the compatibilism debate — as an instance, an analogue, or neither?

what it asks

Compatibilists hold that free will can coexist with determinism, the thesis that every act follows from the laws of nature and the remote past. The question asks whether a constrained will can be free, and whether ASK's bounded discretion, judgment inside a delegated mandate, is an instance of metaphysical freedom as the compatibilism debate understands it (a genuine case), an analogue (a parallel with a stated break) or neither. "Constrained" can mean causal constraint, as in determinism, or authorization bounds set by a delegating authority. When the survey warns that bounded discretion is not unconstrained will, it uses "constrained" in the second sense only.

the main positions

Classical compatibilism begins with Hobbes and Hume, who treat freedom as acting on one's will without external impediment. As the survey reads it, that account supplies no alternatives, so the tradition added a separate conditional analysis: Moore, Hobart and Ayer read "could have done otherwise" as "would have, had she chosen", though Moore declined to treat that as enough for free will. The encyclopedia's "Compatibilism" entry reports that this analysis failed, since the conditional can hold of someone with no real alternative, which the survey reads as a failed analysis, not a refuted compatibilism. The new dispositionalists (Vihvelin, Fara, M. Smith) test ability with the agent's causal base held fixed.

On the categorical reading, van Inwagen's consequence argument, in "The Incompatibility of Free Will and Determinism" (1975), holds that if determinism is true, our acts follow from the laws and the remote past, which are not up to us, so our acts are not up to us. Lewis, in "Are We Free to Break the Laws?" (1981), replies that being able to do something such that a law would have been broken is not being able to break a law.

On the leeway axis, the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP) makes moral responsibility require that one could have done otherwise. Frankfurt, in "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (1969), rejects it with cases where an intervener would force the choice but never acts, so the agent cannot do otherwise yet seems responsible, and the encyclopedia reports no clear victor among the replies. The survey records, as a live dispute, that leeway and sourcehood do not separate as cleanly as the standard taxonomy suggests, and that the PAP sources it read address responsibility for an act, not latitude inside an authorized mandate.

On the sourcehood and hierarchical-control axis, Frankfurt's "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971) makes the will free when the desire that moves you is the one you want to move you, and Fischer and Ravizza require a reasons-responsive mechanism that the agent owns. Manipulation arguments from Pereboom and Mele, which hold that being determined is relevantly like being covertly manipulated, press every compatibilist account.

Ronald Dworkin, in "The Model of Rules" (1967), treats discretion as relative to a standard-setting authority and distinguishes two weak senses, in which the standards demand judgment or the decision is final, from a strong sense, in which the official is not bound by that authority's standards, which he denies is license. The survey records that the text does not address the will.

ASK's Governance doctrine defines delegated discretion, which the survey treats as the definition of bounded discretion, as bounded judgment in his first weak sense, where the standards demand judgment, by an authorized decision-maker within the governing standard and delegated scope. Bounded Generativity adds that weak discretion confers no finality or immunity from review, and that the judging decision-maker may be human or computational, though authorization alone does not suffice and current model sampling does not qualify.

Domingos holds that intelligence and will need not reside in one body so long as a line of control connects them, arguing from an analogy with genes and the organisms they shape that he himself qualifies. In the survey's judgment, surprising behavior does not establish a machine will, and persisting as a lineage, tending to serve one and remaining under unbroken control are different properties.

what ASK ruled

ASK decided this question only for the readings and axes stated:

  • On the conditional-ability reading: yes, a constrained will can be free.
  • On categorical ability, with the past and the laws held fixed: not settled.
  • On the leeway (PAP) axis: bounded discretion is an analogue, with a recorded break.
  • On the sourcehood and hierarchical-control axis: an analogue, with a distinct recorded break.
  • As to instance: no. Bounded discretion is not an instance of metaphysical freedom.

ASK ruled these states and ruled no reason for them. It also ruled that the survey's pairing of bounded discretion with Ronald Dworkin's distinction remains unclassified, with no relation such as instance or analogue assigned to it. The ruling does not call bounded discretion incompatible with metaphysical freedom.

what remains open

ASK left categorical ability unsettled, and the survey records the consequence-argument and Frankfurt-case disputes as unresolved. The survey treats the separability of leeway and sourcehood as live, and finds no relation established among Frankfurt's identification, Ronald Dworkin's weak discretion and bounded discretion, each pair resting on a single unreplicated reading compared over summaries. It also records that the gloss of the strong sense in ASK's normative-apex doctrine, as the authority to set the standard, diverges from Ronald Dworkin's text.

the ruling as recorded

Question 6 // Is the interface/surface the metaphysical boundary between minds, or a rendered relation among agency, delegation, authority, and perceived personhood?

what it asks

Other minds are ordinarily met through a surface: behavior, speech, a screen. The question sets two readings of that surface side by side. On the first, the interface is where one mind really ends and another begins, a boundary in the nature of things. On the second, what appears there is rendered, meaning produced by a set of relations: who is acting, who delegated to whom, who holds authority, and whether an observer perceives a person. The survey records that the question is not itself about who may hold the source-of-intent role, but bears on how that would be argued. It connects to ASK's method idea of a seed-mediated generated interface, in which an AI runtime renders a working interface from a durable seed.

the main positions

No individual source is recorded as evidence on this question, though material with candidate bearing on it exists, as described below. The survey pairs its theme with three candidate literatures: the other-minds problem (how anyone can know that another being has a mind), social cognition (how we come to attribute awareness to others), and perceived agency and the intentional stance (interpreting a system as though it had beliefs and desires). It records that pairing in the list where it sets ASK's ideas beside the literature only as a candidate, because the material behind it has not passed the survey's source-tracing rule.

The consciousness part of the survey does contain material in these literatures. That material was gathered, however, without recording which source supports which statement. Under the survey's rules it may be described and discussed, but no statement from it can carry weight in a recommendation until it has been traced to an exact source, and none has been. The survey's later work on free will, on the links between consciousness and free will, and on machines, personhood and recourse added nothing that could carry weight.

The survey also carries two guards. Explaining when people attribute consciousness shows neither that those attributions are correct nor that they are false. And because a system trained on human reports can reproduce the form of first-person reporting, its fluent reports no longer discriminate and establish neither consciousness nor standing.

ASK's own conclusion, set out separately on this page, holds that behavior at an interface cannot by itself establish the nature of the process producing it, or whether that process is conscious.

what ASK ruled

On August 23, 2026, ASK ruled nothing on the question itself. ASK adopted a three-part statement of the evidence.

  1. Material on interfaces and surfaces exists in the consciousness part of the survey.
  2. It has not passed the survey's rule that each statement must be traced to its source, and it carries no weight in any recommendation.
  3. No admissible statement currently decides whether the interface or surface is a metaphysical boundary between minds.

Because nothing admissible decides it, the whole question stays open. ASK opened no further step on it.

what remains open

The whole question. The survey's own analysis proposed a statement-by-statement tracing pass over a defined set of entries as the next step, if one were opened. That is a recommendation and carries no authority. Tracing the entire consciousness material would mean re-reading its sources, which is new research that nothing authorizes.

the ruling as recorded

ASK conclusion

This section is ASK's own conclusion. The Cogito in its title is Descartes's "I think, therefore I am." ASK wrote it after the survey's program of work had closed, as its own interpretation of the research, distinct from the survey's findings. It alters none of the survey's findings and changes the disposition of none of the six questions, so it is not a ruling on any of them.

ASK argues that consciousness can be known with certainty only from the inside, by the one having the experience, and that everyone else can attribute it only through evidence and a theory linking outward signs to inner experience. So when no outside test settles the matter, consciousness has not been established to the observer, which is a different thing from its being absent.

where the surveyed literature meets this argument▼

This block reports the survey's material, not ASK's. It names positions the research recorded on the same ground. ASK's conclusion names none of these sources, and the research records no link between them and it. Placing them here is this page's arrangement.

Ned Block distinguishes phenomenal consciousness, what an experience is like for the one having it, from access consciousness, a state whose content is poised for use in reasoning and in the rational control of action and speech, a definition he later revised. He presents this as a conceptual distinction and takes no side on whether everything is physical. The survey's assessment is that it obliges anyone arguing from consciousness to name the sense meant, without settling any conclusion.

David Chalmers's hard problem asks why functions such as report and the control of behavior are accompanied by experience at all. He frames it as a point about explanation and does not claim to show that physicalism, the view that everything is physical, is false. It descends from Joseph Levine's explanatory gap and from Thomas Nagel's argument that a creature is conscious when there is something it is like to be that creature. Daniel Dennett, who rejects the hard problem, proposes heterophenomenology, a method that treats a subject's reports and behavior as data from which an observer builds a theory of how things seem to that subject.

Michael Graziano's attention schema theory explains how a system comes to claim awareness, by building a simplified model of its own attention, and Graziano disclaims explaining how we have experience. The survey's assessment is that the theory gives a non-conscious system a route to sincere reports of being conscious, which makes such reports less evidential. It also holds that tests validated on human reports do not carry over to systems whose outputs are modeled on them.

Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and colleagues propose indicator properties, drawn from several scientific theories, whose presence makes consciousness more likely. The list is provisional and rests on computational functionalism, the hypothesis that the right computations suffice for consciousness, which the authors adopt as a disputed working hypothesis. Jonathan Birch names a gaming problem. Where a system can mimic the behaviors most likely to persuade people of its sentience, its capacity for felt experience, without having that capacity, behavioral tests stop being reliable. The survey records that none of the source records it obtained contains an empirical judgment of whether a specific current AI system is conscious, a bound on what was read rather than a finding about the literature.

In clinical work, Y. G. Bodien and colleagues, in "Cognitive Motor Dissociation in Disorders of Consciousness" (2024), found covert command-following, called cognitive motor dissociation, in 60 of 241 participants who showed no observable response to commands, so unresponsiveness is an unreliable sign that cognition is absent. As the survey reads the clinical literature on this dissociation, it makes the inference from unresponsiveness to unconsciousness invalid without saying how often unresponsive patients are in fact conscious, and the opposite error, inferring consciousness from any preserved brain activity, remains a live risk.

David Deutsch and Steven Pinker's disagreement over whether general creative intelligence brings experience, set out under Question 1, also touches this ground. In their 2023 dialogue Deutsch adds that the physical substrate makes no difference in principle, and Pinker replies that the brain's biochemistry might be what supplies experience. In the survey's judgment, Pinker's objection establishes no substrate requirement.

The survey's limits, set out under what the survey covers, apply here, in particular the likely over-representation of illusionism.

ASK prospective axis

The conclusion asks whether anyone is home in a system. ASK's prospective axis asks what follows if someone is: whether the same subject survives being paused and resumed, copied, split or combined, and which rights follow the subject, the branch, the lineage or the office. ASK presents it as a question-space the research opened and did not survey. The research has not read the philosophical literature on personal identity, and ASK claims no novelty against it.

For the sake of argument, the axis grants that an artificial system is a conscious subject and, separately, wherever it speaks of welfare, that things can matter to that subject. Neither grant is a claim about any actual system, and neither covers legal status or governance. The axis is not a seventh question, and it settles nothing.

positions recorded beside the axis▼

This block is separate from ASK's text. It names positions in the survey's evidence record that touch nearby ground. They neither answer, test nor fill in the axis, which remains unsurveyed. The survey records these works as bearing on other questions on this page, and ASK has placed none of them in the axis.

On legal personhood, Joanna Bryson, Mihailis Diamantis and Thomas Grant, in "Of, for, and by the people: the legal lacuna of synthetic persons" (2017), argue that legal personality is conferred by law and, being a bundle of rights and obligations, can be divided. Simon Chesterman, in "Artificial Intelligence and the Limits of Legal Personality" (2020), holds instead that legal personality is either recognized or not, though its content can vary, and the survey records the two as directly disagreeing on that point. Ugo Pagallo separates legal agency, such as acting as an agent in contract law, from legal personhood, and rejects the inference in either direction. The survey found no source in this area claiming that moral standing brings legal standing.

Two works the survey records under other questions touch this ground. David Deutsch, in The Beginning of Infinity, forecasts that backups of brain states will prevent deaths by homicide or accident, and holds that a person simulated on a computer must be a person while a recording of the simulation is not. Pedro Domingos, in The Master Algorithm (Basic Books, 2015), reports a proposal he attributes to some researchers, to copy what one robot's mind has learned into further robots. In the survey's judgment, recovery from a backup has not been shown to be survival, the personhood of a simulation is Deutsch's own position, and the book gives no sufficient bridge from learning to experience and does not show the copies to be one and the same subject.

the evidence

The survey keeps a record for each source it relies on and for each lead it has located, which the map calls an evidence owner. A record identifies the work and, where captured, its edition, the kind of source and whether it is open to read or paywalled. Almost every record carries two grades, and the survey keeps them apart because they answer different questions.

The first grade says how the survey reached the work. A source read directly was read in its own text, and the grade covers only claims inside the stretch actually read, which may be a single chapter. A source read through an intermediary was reached through something standing between the survey and the work, such as an automated extraction, a review or a published transcript. It normally supports paraphrase only. A lead is a work the survey has located but not read, and it carries no weight. The grade belongs to a particular reading and the passage it covered, so a direct read of one chapter does not raise the grade of the rest of the book. As the survey notes, these differences in depth reflect access, not the rigor of the reading. The free-will literature, much of it openly published, was read directly far more often than the consciousness literature.

The second grade says whether the source's wording may be quoted. Wording is quotable only where the whole work was read directly, so that any passage can be shown to lie inside what was read. Where the direct read covered only part of a work, or the copy read has not been checked against the published edition, a quotation must be re-verified against the source before it is used. Everything else is paraphrase only.

The survey's reason for keeping quotation apart from paraphrase is its own record of failure. It documents three independent breakdowns in the extraction layer it worked through, one of them an automated summary that invented a claim of neutrality. It also found records claiming exact wording that their own grade showed had come through a summary. Where the two conflict, the grade governs, and nothing that passed through a summary counts as an author's words until it has been checked. Apart from ASK's own published text and a few fixed phrases, this page paraphrases its sources.

The map, which the page header calls a typed relation atlas, draws the survey's objects: its positions, theories, claims and findings, ASK's pairings of its own ideas with the literature, and the six questions. Sources appear in the inspector, the panel that opens when an object is selected. The map shows three kinds of link. Registered relations are typed relations the survey has entered between objects, such as one claim conflicting with, requiring or challenging another, or bearing on a held question. There are eight, all running from the survey's claims about consciousness, and they are always drawn, with their direction. Recorded references are links written into a structured field of a record, such as the questions a source bears on or the sources a free-will position rests on. They are not registered relations, and the map shows them only when you select an object. Unconnected context, labeled unedged context on the map, covers prose bearing, analogy and navigation, and is never drawn. Sources never appear as points on the map. They sit in the inspector.
